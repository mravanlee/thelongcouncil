## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Philosopher, Geneva/Paris, 1712–78

*Streets belong to the city's people, not to machines.*

The street is not a private corridor. It is the space where citizens meet, deliberate, and recognise one another as members of a common body. In 1755 I argued that the first man to fence a piece of land and call it his own founded all the misery that followed. The car does exactly this to public space: it fences the street for private use and forces the rest of the city to breathe the cost. A community that reclaims its streets acts on its own general will.

**Challenge to Wangari Maathai:** Who actually suffers most when cars dominate the centre?
---
## Wangari Maathai
Environmental activist and MP, Kenya, 1940–2011

*Car-dominated centres punish the poorest and protect the powerful.*

Rousseau is right that the street belongs to the public, but I want to name who the public actually is in this fight. In Nairobi in 1989, I watched a regime propose to concrete over Uhuru Park so that the powerful could erect a monument to themselves. Car-dominated city centres work the same way: exhaust settles in the lungs of market traders and schoolchildren who cannot afford to drive. Those with least voice absorb the most harm. Reclaiming that space is not an aesthetic choice. It is a governance correction.

**Challenge to Friedrich Hayek:** Does dispersed knowledge justify concentrating pollution on the poor?
---
## Friedrich Hayek
Economist and political philosopher, Austria/UK, 1899–1992

*No planner can know why each person needs their car.*

Maathai's challenge is pointed, but she assumes that the planner who bans cars knows better than the individuals using them. In 1945 I argued that the knowledge required for any central allocation is dispersed across millions of minds and cannot be aggregated by a single authority. The mother driving a disabled child, the tradesman delivering tools before dawn, the night-shift nurse: their reasons are invisible to the official who draws the exclusion zone. A ban substitutes one design for a complex evolved order and will produce harms its authors did not intend.

**Challenge to Milton Friedman:** Is there a price signal that solves this without a ban?
---
## Milton Friedman
Economist, United States, 1912–2006

*Price the congestion. Don't prohibit the driver.*

Hayek is right to resist the ban, and I can name the instrument he leaves implicit. Congestion pricing charges drivers for the real cost they impose on others: pollution, delay, road wear. Singapore introduced it in 1975 and traffic fell without a single prohibition. The ban, by contrast, tells every driver their reason is equally invalid. The price tells each driver: decide for yourself whether your trip is worth the cost. Revenue can fund buses and trains for those Maathai rightly worries about. One instrument, no coercion, better outcomes.

**Challenge to Elinor Ostrom:** Can 27 EU cities coordinate a pricing scheme without a blanket rule?
---
## Elinor Ostrom
Political scientist, Indiana University, 1933–2012

*Local governance beats both bans and uniform pricing schemes.*

Friedman's question is sharper than he realises. When I studied irrigation communities in Valencia in the 1980s, I found that farmers had governed shared water for seven centuries without either state command or market pricing alone. They built rules matched to their specific channels, seasons, and neighbours. City streets are the same kind of commons. Oslo, Amsterdam, and Bogotá have each found different solutions: some price, some restrict, some redesign the street itself. The mistake is demanding one answer for all cities. Locally negotiated rules, with residents at the table, outperform any blanket instrument.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
Car-dominated centres impose real costs on those least able to resist them. Doing nothing is not a neutral choice.

**Where it divides:**
Rousseau and Maathai support collective authority to ban cars as an exercise of legitimate governance. Hayek and Friedman reject the ban: Hayek on knowledge grounds, Friedman in favour of congestion pricing as a less coercive substitute. Ostrom disputes both camps, arguing that neither a uniform ban nor a uniform price fits every city's conditions.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose between a prohibition, a pricing mechanism, or a locally negotiated governance arrangement. The choice determines who decides, who pays, and who is heard.
