## John Rawls
Thinker, United States, 1921–2002

*Inequality is only justified when it lifts the worst-off.*

Design the distribution from behind a veil of ignorance: not knowing whether you will be born rich or poor, you would never gamble with the bottom. In 1971 I argued that inequalities are permissible only when they make the least advantaged better off than any more equal arrangement would. That is not equality of outcome. It is a constraint on what inequality may do. A society that fails this test is not merely inefficient. It is unjust.

**Challenge to Friedrich Hayek:** Who speaks for the worst-off when no one planned the outcome?

## Friedrich Hayek
Economist and political philosopher, Austria/United Kingdom, 1899–1992

*No agent designed the outcome, so no agent can correct it.*

Rawls asks who speaks for the worst-off. The market already does, more effectively than any planner can. In my 1945 essay on the use of knowledge in society I showed that prices aggregate millions of fragments of local, tacit information that no central authority can possess. To impose a pattern on this process destroys the very mechanism that generates prosperity. I support a guaranteed income floor. What I oppose is the pretence that anyone can design a just distribution from above.

**Challenge to Milton Friedman:** What floor is compatible with market incentives intact?
---
## Milton Friedman
Economist, United States, 1912–2006

*A cash floor preserves freedom; a welfare bureaucracy destroys it.*

Hayek asks the right question. In 1962 I answered it: a negative income tax. Every household below a threshold receives a direct cash transfer, phased out as income rises. No housing officials, no food inspectors, no caseworkers deciding who deserves what. The cash preserves choice. Rawls wants the worst-off to benefit from inequality; I agree, but the instrument matters. Bureaucratic redistribution replaces one dependency with another. A clean cash floor does not.

**Challenge to Amartya Sen:** Does cash actually reach what people need to live well?
---
## Amartya Sen
Economist and philosopher, India/United Kingdom/United States, born 1933

*Income alone cannot measure whether people are actually free.*

Friedman's cash floor is better than a bureaucratic maze, but it answers the wrong question. In the 1943 Bengal famine, food existed in the province. People starved because they lacked entitlement to it. Cash solves an entitlement problem only if markets for health, education, and basic security already function. They often do not. A person with a disability, or a woman in a household where she controls nothing, converts the same income into far less real freedom. Distribution must target capabilities, not just currency.

**Challenge to Rosa Luxemburg:** Can redistribution fix what the structure of ownership produces?
---
## Rosa Luxemburg
Revolutionary theorist, Poland/Germany, 1871–1919

*Redistribution within capitalism cannot undo what capitalism continuously makes.*

Sen asks whether redistribution can fix structural ownership. It cannot, and the history of European social democracy by 1913 had already shown why. The mechanism of capitalist accumulation does not merely produce inequality as a side effect. It reproduces it as a condition of its own survival. I watched the German SPD negotiate wages and win reforms, while the relations of production that generated exploitation remained untouched. Rawls's difference principle, Friedman's cash floor, and Sen's capabilities all operate within the system whose logic they leave intact.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
All five members accept that a pure market outcome, left entirely uncorrected, produces a floor that is morally or practically unacceptable. Even Hayek and Friedman support a guaranteed minimum.

**Where it divides:**
Rawls and Sen argue the metric must go beyond income, to opportunity and capability respectively. Hayek and Friedman insist the instrument must preserve market signals. Luxemburg breaks from all four: she holds that redistribution within capitalist ownership structures is structurally self-defeating, placing her outside the reform debate entirely.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose between a direct cash floor with minimal conditions, a capability-targeted programme covering health and education, or structural reform of asset ownership. Each implies a different theory of why inequality persists.
