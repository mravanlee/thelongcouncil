## John Locke
Philosopher, England, 1632–1704

*Resistance requires systematic tyranny, not scattered grievance.*

In the Second Treatise I set the bar high on purpose. Government dissolves itself only through long, deliberate abuse that reveals a settled design against the people's rights, not through single unpopular laws. A democratically elected government complicates this further: it carries the people's own consent built into it. Civil disobedience against such a government is justified only when that consent has been systematically betrayed, when the ballot itself has become a cover for tyranny rather than its remedy.

**Challenge to Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** Can an elected government still betray the general will?
---
## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Philosopher, Geneva, 1712–78

*Elections can mask the general will, not guarantee it.*

Yes, easily. I wrote that the English believed themselves free merely because they elected Parliament, then spent the rest of their lives enslaved to it. A vote every few years is not sovereignty. If an elected government serves factions, the wealthy, parties, foreign interests, rather than the common good, it has broken the one thing that made it legitimate. Disobedience is justified then. But I warn my own table: whoever claims to speak the general will against the government can also become the new tyranny.

**Challenge to Rosa Luxemburg:** Does structural exclusion make elections themselves the fraud?
---
## Rosa Luxemburg
Revolutionary theorist, Germany, 1871–1919

*Elections that preserve class rule deserve mass resistance.*

Precisely that. In 1899 I told Bernstein that reform cannot touch capitalism's mechanism, only soften its surface, and elected parliaments under capitalist property relations reproduce exclusion by design. When the ballot box consistently protects capital against labour, the mass strike is not an attack on democracy but democracy's own working-class expression. Yet I learned something Rousseau did not live to see: in 1918 I warned Lenin's Bolsheviks that suppressing dissent in the name of the common good produces bureaucracy, not freedom. Disobedience must stay democratic or it devours itself.

**Challenge to Hannah Arendt:** Can mass action stay power without becoming violence?
---
## Hannah Arendt
Political philosopher, Germany/USA, 1906–75

*Only public, collective, nonviolent disobedience generates legitimate power.*

It can, and Luxemburg's mass strike is close to what I mean by power: people acting together in concert, not force applied by some against others. In 1970 I distinguished power from violence precisely because they are opposites. A government ruling only through coercion has already lost the consent that made it powerful. Civil disobedience, open, collective, answerable to the public, addresses the conscience of fellow citizens rather than destroying them. It is democracy correcting itself in public, not democracy's enemy breaking in from outside.

**Challenge to John Rawls:** When has a nearly just society actually failed that test?
---
## John Rawls
Political philosopher, United States, 1921–2002

*Only clear, substantial injustice in an otherwise just order justifies breaking law.*

Arendt is right that disobedience must stay public and nonviolent, but I add a further condition: it belongs only to a nearly just society, one whose constitution is basically sound but has failed in some serious, identifiable way. Segregation statutes failed that test; ordinary policy disagreement does not. The actor must have tried normal channels, courts, elections, petitions, and exhausted them first. Without that restraint, disobedience stops appealing to the public's sense of justice and becomes simply one more faction imposing its will by other means.

**Challenge to Nelson Mandela:** What happens when normal channels were never open at all?

---
## Nelson Mandela
President, South Africa, 1994–99

*When all peaceful doors are shut, resistance escalates, then negotiates.*

Rawls assumes channels exist to exhaust. In South Africa they did not: no vote, no court, no petition reached a black citizen under apartheid law. That is why in 1961 I helped found Umkhonto we Sizwe only after the Sharpeville massacre and a banned ANC closed every legal road. But I also chose, in 1990, to suspend armed struggle unilaterally, because once negotiation became possible, escalation past that point would have destroyed the legitimacy we needed to govern afterward. Justified resistance must know when to stop.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
All six agree disobedience needs a real, demonstrated failure of justice, not mere disagreement with a policy outcome. Arendt, Luxemburg and Mandela agree legitimate resistance must stay collective and answerable to the public.

**Where it divides:**
Locke and Rawls set a high threshold inside functioning institutions; Luxemburg and Rousseau argue elections themselves can be the fraud. Mandela splits from Rawls by showing some governments offer no channels to exhaust at all.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Decide whether your institutions are "nearly just" with open channels, as Rawls requires, or structurally closed, as Mandela and Luxemburg describe. That assessment determines whether lawful protest or escalation is the legitimate next step.
