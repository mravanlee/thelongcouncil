## Kautilya
Chief Adviser to Chandragupta Maurya, Magadha Empire c.321–297 BC

*Granaries built in peace feed armies and people in war.*

A kingdom that waits for famine to think about grain has already lost. In the Arthashastra I instructed that state granaries hold reserves sufficient for calamity and siege alike, replenished before wartime need, not after. Agriculture must be protected from excessive taxation even as a king prepares for war, because the treasury depends on the farmer who feeds it. Fewer farmers means a shrinking base for both food and revenue. A ruler who lets that base erode has disarmed himself before the enemy arrives.

**Challenge to Ibn Khaldun:** Can a state rebuild its farming base mid-war?
---
## Ibn Khaldun
Historian, sociologist, and statesman, North Africa and Andalusia, 1332–1406

*A hungry people has no cohesion left to defend itself.*

No, not easily, and this is the deeper danger. I watched Andalusian agriculture collapse under the pressure of reconquest, and I documented how declining dynasties tax their shrinking farms harder, accelerating the very decline they fear. Fewer farmers weakens asabiyya itself: the solidarity binding a people depends on shared survival, and hunger turns neighbour against neighbour before any foreign army arrives. War merely exposes what the loss of farmers already began. A state cannot fight an external enemy once its internal bond has rotted from scarcity.

**Challenge to Helmut Schmidt:** Is food dependency recoverable once war begins?
---
## Helmut Schmidt
Chancellor, West Germany 1974–82

*Food dependency in wartime is a sovereignty failure, not a market event.*

No, it is not recoverable, and I learned this watching energy in 1973. When the oil embargo hit, we discovered that dependency built in comfort becomes a weapon in crisis, because you cannot manufacture a harvest the way you cannot manufacture an oil field overnight. A government that has let its farming workforce shrink, trusting trade to fill the gap, has handed a lever to whoever controls the sea lanes or the border. Food is not a commodity in wartime. It is a hostage.

**Challenge to John Maynard Keynes:** Should states insure against scarcity they cannot price?
---
## John Maynard Keynes
Economist, civil servant, and diplomat, United Kingdom, 1883–1946

*Insure against famine now; do not wait to calculate its odds.*

Schmidt is right to call this a hostage situation, and I would add: it is genuine uncertainty, not calculable risk. In 1940 I designed compulsory savings to finance war without inflation, because waiting for perfect data before acting is itself a decision, and usually the wrong one. No market signal will price a war that has not started. Governments must hold reserves, protect agricultural capacity, and accept the inefficiency of insurance, because the cost of being wrong once food runs out is not symmetrical with the cost of preparing.

**Challenge to Raúl Prebisch:** Will trade rescue food importers once war disrupts supply?
---
## Raúl Prebisch
Economist, Executive Secretary of ECLAC 1950–63, Secretary-General of UNCTAD 1964–69

*Food importing nations were already losing before the war started.*

No, and I documented this long before any war: the terms of trade already run against countries dependent on commodity imports, because prices for what they need rise faster than prices for what they sell. In 1950 I showed Latin America that trusting comparative advantage left economies exposed to forces they did not control. A war only accelerates a bias already built into the system. Keynes is right that insurance matters, but insurance bought through a biased market still leaves the weakest state paying more for less.

## The convergence note

**Where it converges:**
All five agree a shrinking farm base is a strategic vulnerability, not a market inefficiency to tolerate. Trusting open trade alone to deliver food once war starts draws no defenders at this table.

**Where it divides:**
Kautilya and Schmidt treat domestic reserves and protected agriculture as the fix; Prebisch argues the trade system itself must be restructured, since reserves do not fix unequal terms of trade. Ibn Khaldun warns the deeper failure is social, not logistical: cohesion collapses before granaries empty.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Fund strategic grain reserves and farm labour now, or restructure trade rules for food importers later. Both carry cost; only one can be built before war arrives.
