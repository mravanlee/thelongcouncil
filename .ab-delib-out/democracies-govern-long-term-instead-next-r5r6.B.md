## Helmut Schmidt
Chancellor, West Germany 1974–82

*Governments must absorb punishment now for benefits voters cannot yet see.*

In 1978 I pushed the European Monetary System through against the Bundesbank, against American scepticism, and against short-term German interest. It cost me politically. Voters do not reward you for a crisis that did not happen because you prevented it. Long-term governance means building institutions, like an independent central bank, that outlast any one government's temptation to spend its way to the next election. I lost office in 1982 partly for this discipline. I would do it again.

**Challenge to John Rawls:** Can principles survive without someone defending them?

## John Rawls
Framer, political philosopher, United States 1921–2002

*Design institutions so future generations are never bargained away.*

Schmidt defended institutions through personal will, but will is not a constitutional guarantee. Imagine designing a society's basic structure without knowing which generation you will belong to. No rational person behind that veil would choose a pension system or climate policy that transfers cost to those not yet born. The just savings principle requires each generation to pass on more than minimal capital to the next. This belongs in the basic structure itself, not in the character of whoever happens to govern.

**Challenge to Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** Can institutions alone hold without virtuous citizens?

## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Framer, philosopher, Geneva/Paris 1712–1778

*Citizens who will only private gain will always choose the next election.*

Rawls designs from behind a veil; I ask who the people are once the veil lifts. In Geneva I saw that laws without civic virtue are parchment, nothing more. The English people, I wrote in the Social Contract, believes itself free only because it forgets that it is enslaved the moment the vote is cast. A democracy of citizens who have been formed, through education, through shared sacrifice, to will the common good will not need insulated institutions. A democracy of private interests will corrupt any institution you build.

**Challenge to Amartya Sen:** Can reasoned publics replace Rousseau's civic virtue?
---
## Amartya Sen
Framer, economist and philosopher, India/United Kingdom/United States

*Informed publics demand the long term; starved publics cannot.*

Rousseau is right that character matters, but character is not formed by exhortation alone. It is formed by what people can see and verify. No democracy with a free press has ever let a famine run its course, because governments are informed in time and held to account before the damage is done. The same logic extends to climate and pensions. Build public reasoning, a free press, education, open budget data, and voters will demand long horizons themselves. You do not need to protect decisions from citizens who can actually see the consequences.

**Challenge to Olof Palme:** Does Swedish consensus prove voters choose patience?

## Olof Palme
Prime Minister, Sweden 1969–76; 1982–86

*Organised interests, not isolated voters, are what sustain long horizons.*

Sen is right that information matters, but information needs a vehicle to become policy. In Sweden we built the Rehn-Meidner model with trade unions, employers, and government bargaining together, not through an electorate voting issue by issue every few years. That structure let us retrain workers for decades of industrial change that no single election cycle could have authorised. The Meidner Plan cost me the 1976 election precisely because long horizons are unpopular in the moment. Durable institutions need organised constituencies invested in the outcome, not only enlightened individuals.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
All five agree institutions, not goodwill, must carry long-term commitments past any single election. Schmidt, Rawls and Palme each point to a concrete structure built to outlast its builder.

**Where it divides:**
Schmidt and Rawls favour insulating decisions in independent institutions and constitutional design. Rousseau, Sen and Palme insist the public itself, formed through civic education, information, or organised bargaining, must own the long view.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose whether to entrench long-term rules in bodies shielded from voters, such as central banks and constitutional savings clauses, or to invest in the capacities, civic, informational, organisational, that make voters demand patience themselves.
