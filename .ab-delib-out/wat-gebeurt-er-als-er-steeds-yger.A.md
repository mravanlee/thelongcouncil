## Kautilya
Framer, Chief Adviser to Chandragupta Maurya, Magadha c.321–297 BC

*A state without grain reserves has already surrendered before war begins.*

When Chandragupta unified the subcontinent, the first administrative act I designed was not the army. It was the granary. The Arthashastra specifies state-managed grain storage, irrigation investment, and agricultural labour protections precisely because a ruler who cannot feed soldiers cannot fight, and a ruler who cannot feed subjects cannot govern. Fewer farmers means thinner reserves. Thinner reserves in wartime means famine before the enemy arrives. The treasury follows the harvest; everything else follows the treasury.

**Challenge to Ibn Khaldun:** When farmers vanish, does the state's cohesion vanish with them?

---
## Ibn Khaldun
Framer, Historian and Statesman, North Africa and Egypt 1332–1406

*Losing farmers means losing the social bond that holds states together.*

Yes, Kautilya, it does. I watched the Hafsids in Tunis and the Marinids in Morocco run the same sequence: tax the farmers hard, watch them abandon the land, then discover that the soldiers who were supposed to defend the state no longer had a food base to defend. In 1401, when Timur besieged Damascus, the city's granaries were already thin from years of rural flight. External war does not destroy a state. It reveals a state already destroyed from within.

**Challenge to Helmut Schmidt:** Is food dependency the same sovereignty trap as energy dependency?

---
## Helmut Schmidt
Chancellor, West Germany 1974–82

*Food dependency and energy dependency are the same strategic failure.*

It is exactly the same trap. In November 1973, when OPEC cut supply, I told the Bundestag that energy dependence was not an energy question but a sovereignty question. You cannot make that argument about oil and then ignore wheat. A government that has allowed its domestic agricultural base to erode, trusting foreign supply chains to hold in a crisis, has made itself hostage. Supply chains do not hold in wartime. A statesman who cannot imagine that scenario should not be making irreversible agricultural policy.

**Challenge to Keynes:** Should governments insure against food scarcity or optimise for normal times?

---
## John Maynard Keynes
Framer, Economist and Treasury Official, United Kingdom 1883–1946

*Under genuine uncertainty, insure against the worst outcome, not the likely one.*

Schmidt puts the question precisely. In 1940, writing on how to pay for the war, I distinguished between conditions where you can calculate probabilities and conditions where you cannot. Wartime food scarcity is the second kind. You cannot assign a reliable probability to when the next war will come or how long it will last. That means optimising for peacetime efficiency in agriculture is the wrong policy frame. The correct frame is insurance: what is the worst plausible outcome, and what does it cost to make it survivable?

**Challenge to Raúl Prebisch:** Will global markets actually deliver food to poor countries when scarcity hits?

---
## Raúl Prebisch
Framer, Executive Secretary ECLAC 1950–63; Secretary-General UNCTAD 1964–69

*Markets will not deliver food to the periphery when the centre needs it first.*

Keynes asks the right question, and the answer is no. Managing Argentina's Central Bank through the 1930s Depression taught me that commodity-dependent countries are price-takers, not price-setters. In a war-induced scarcity, the terms of trade collapse for food-importing developing countries: prices rise, foreign exchange drains, and wealthy nations outbid them on every available shipment. The international trading system was not designed to serve the periphery in normal times. In a crisis, it actively works against them. Domestic food capacity is not inefficiency. It is survival insurance that markets will never voluntarily provide.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
All five members agree that domestic food production capacity is a sovereignty asset, not merely an economic variable. All five agree that markets will not reliably supply food to vulnerable populations during wartime scarcity.

**Where it divides:**
Keynes and Prebisch agree on the insurance logic but split on mechanism: Keynes frames it as government policy under uncertainty; Prebisch argues the international system is structurally rigged and requires institutional reform, not just domestic reserves. Kautilya and Ibn Khaldun see agricultural decline as a civilisational warning sign; Schmidt sees it as a strategic policy failure correctable by government decision.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose between building domestic food resilience through strategic reserves and protected agricultural capacity, or reforming international food trade rules so that poor countries can access global supplies during a crisis. These are not mutually exclusive, but they require different budgets, different institutions, and different timelines.
