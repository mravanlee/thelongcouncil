## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Philosopher, Geneva/Paris, 1712–78

*Unequal citizens cannot share a common political purpose.*

When one man owns what a thousand need, the vote becomes a ceremony. I watched Geneva's merchant oligarchs perform exactly this ritual — elections held, deliberations staged, outcomes predetermined by property. Political polarization is real, but ask what feeds it. Citizens who share roughly equal material stakes can disagree fiercely and still recognize one another as members of the same republic. Strip that equality away and you do not get debate. You get factions performing war.

**Challenge to Hannah Arendt:** Can shared facts survive when material stakes diverge so sharply?

---
## Hannah Arendt
Political philosopher, Germany/United States, 1906–75

*Shared reality collapses before inequality does its worst.*

Rousseau asks what feeds polarization. I answer: the destruction of a common world precedes economic grievance in the causal chain. I watched Germany in the 1930s. The Weimar Republic had poverty and inequality in abundance, but what finished it was the collapse of a shared public realm — the moment citizens stopped arguing about the same facts and started inhabiting separate realities. Atomized people do not organize against the wealthy. They surrender to whoever offers them a tribe.

**Challenge to John Rawls:** Can institutional design hold when the public world has already fractured?

---
## John Rawls
Political philosopher, United States, 1921–2002

*The two threats are one spiral, not two rivals.*

Arendt is right that a fractured public world is lethal, and Rousseau is right that extreme inequality destroys common purpose. But they describe the same spiral from different entry points. The basic structure of society is the primary subject of justice. When that structure allows wealth to concentrate without benefiting the least advantaged, it corrodes the public reason on which political legitimacy depends. Citizens who cannot trust institutions as fair stop reasoning together. Each failure feeds the other.

**Challenge to Rosa Luxemburg:** Is this spiral reformable within existing institutions, or structurally beyond them?

---
## Rosa Luxemburg
Revolutionary theorist, Germany/Poland, 1871–1919

*Polarization is the symptom; class power is the disease.*

Rawls asks whether the spiral is reformable. I watched the SPD vote war credits in August 1914 — a party built to represent workers, captured so thoroughly by bourgeois nationalist pressure that it sent those workers to die. That was not a failure of public reason. It was concentrated economic power purchasing political institutions wholesale. When capital controls parties, media, and legislative agendas, what looks like cultural war between citizens is class conflict with its true name removed. Reform the symptom and the disease advances.

**Challenge to Olof Palme:** Sweden reformed within capitalism — but did it hold?

---
## Olof Palme
Prime Minister, Sweden, 1969–76 and 1982–86

*Redistribution reduces the grievances that polarization feeds on.*

Luxemburg's challenge is fair, and I will not pretend Sweden solved everything. But in the 1970s, the Rehn-Meidner model delivered full employment, compressed wages, and universal social insurance simultaneously. Swedish workers did not fill the streets with tribal rage because the material conditions for that rage were systematically reduced. Polarization is not simply manufactured by elites to distract the poor. It finds genuine purchase where people feel genuinely abandoned. Sustained redistribution removes that purchase. The Nordic evidence is not a fantasy; it is a governance record.

**Challenge to Albert O. Hirschman:** When the wealthy exit shared institutions, does redistribution still work?

---
## Albert O. Hirschman
Political economist, Germany/United States, 1915–2012

*Elite exit from shared institutions is the mechanism linking both threats.*

Palme's question is the right one, and it exposes the mechanism the others have circled without naming. In Colombia in the 1950s I watched elites build private schools, private clinics, and private security the moment public alternatives deteriorated. Exit suppresses voice. When the wealthy leave public hospitals, public schools, and public spaces, they stop having any stake in improving them. Inequality enables that exit. The exit produces the institutional decay. The decay generates the polarization. Separate the mechanism from the outcome and you can intervene.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
Inequality and polarization are not independent threats; each accelerates the other through shared mechanisms of institutional decay and loss of common purpose.

**Where it divides:**
Luxemburg insists economic structure is causally prior and reform within capitalism cannot address it; Palme's governance record directly contests this. Arendt holds that the collapse of shared reality is an autonomous threat, not reducible to material conditions; Rousseau and Luxemburg both treat that collapse as downstream of inequality.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose whether to prioritize redistribution and institutional investment as the primary intervention, or treat the collapse of shared epistemic ground as a separate emergency requiring its own direct response — because the sequencing determines which tools you deploy first.
