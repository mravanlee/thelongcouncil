## Elinor Ostrom
Professor of Political Science, Indiana University, 1933–2012

*Street space is a commons; the rule should fit the street.*

A blanket ban treats every city center as identical, and it is not. In my case studies of irrigation systems in Spain and fisheries in Turkey, the arrangements that lasted were designed with the users, not imposed on them. Some centers have the density and transit alternatives to go car-free tomorrow. Others do not, and a ban without that capacity just pushes congestion to the next ring road. The design question is boundaries, monitoring, and graduated rules, not prohibition by decree.

**Challenge to Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** Can decree ever substitute for negotiated consent?
---
## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Citizen of Geneva, Philosopher, 1712–1778

*The street belongs to the public, not to private carriages.*

Decree and consent are not opposites when the decree expresses what citizens would will for themselves as a community. In Geneva I watched carriages of the wealthy claim the narrow streets while ordinary citizens pressed themselves against the walls. A city center is common ground, and its purpose is the common good, not the sum of private conveniences. If the people, deliberating as a body, judge that reclaiming the square for walking, markets, and meeting serves them better than private driving, that judgment is sovereign, not arbitrary.

**Challenge to Wangari Maathai:** Does reclaiming space always serve the powerless, not just the comfortable?
---
## Wangari Maathai
Founder, Green Belt Movement, Kenya, 1940–2011

*Not always, unless the poor help design the ban.*

In Nairobi I fought Moi's government when it tried to pave over Uhuru Park for a monument to power, and I learned that public space is seized by whoever controls the decision, not whoever claims to serve the public. A car ban designed by downtown elites can displace street vendors and matatu drivers who have no other livelihood. The street must be reclaimed with the people who depend on it, not for them. Otherwise one form of capture simply replaces another.

**Challenge to Friedrich Hayek:** Doesn't planning fail worse when imposed without the poor's voice?

---
## Friedrich Hayek
Economist, Austria/United Kingdom, 1899–1992

*No planner knows why each driver needs that street.*

I agree that imposed plans fail, and this is the deeper reason why. The delivery driver, the night-shift nurse, the disabled shopper, each carries knowledge of their own circumstances that no city council can aggregate into a map. In Vienna I watched planners assume they could redesign complex urban life from an office; they could not. A ban substitutes one designer's judgment for millions of dispersed judgments. The order of a living city, like a market, emerges from use, not decree, however well intentioned the decree.

**Challenge to Milton Friedman:** Is prohibition ever better than pricing the externality?

---
## Milton Friedman
Economist, United States, 1912–2006

*Price the congestion; don't prohibit the car.*

Prohibition is the blunt instrument of a government that distrusts its own citizens to respond to signals. A ban cannot distinguish the retiree visiting a doctor from the commuter who could easily take a bus. Congestion pricing can. Charge drivers for the real cost they impose on others, the same discipline I would apply to any externality, and let the price do the sorting. Revenue funds transit, roads clear, and no bureaucrat decides by decree who may enter the city center.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
All five agree current car-dominated centers often serve the powerful over the poor. All favor local, participatory design over a single imposed national template.

**Where it divides:**
Rousseau and Maathai treat a collectively willed ban as potentially legitimate if the community decides it; Hayek and Friedman reject prohibition itself, preferring price signals over decree. Ostrom sits between, judging by local design conditions rather than principle.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose between outright prohibition, congestion pricing, or a negotiated local governance arrangement with graduated rules. Then decide who sits at the table that designs it.
