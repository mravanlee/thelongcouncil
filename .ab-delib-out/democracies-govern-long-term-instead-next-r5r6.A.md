## John Rawls
Philosopher, United States 1971–2001

*Institutions must protect future generations, not just present majorities.*

The basic structure of society is the primary subject of justice. That structure includes the rules governing time. If we designed electoral institutions from behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing whether we would live in 2024 or 2074, we would never accept a system that systematically discounts future people's interests to serve present voters. We would build independent bodies, constitutional constraints, and long-horizon budgeting rules that no single parliament could dismantle. The problem is not democracy itself. It is that we have built democratic institutions without asking what rational people would choose if they did not know when they would live.

**Challenge to Helmut Schmidt:** Can leaders absorb punishment for correct decisions alone?

## Helmut Schmidt
Chancellor, West Germany 1974–82

*Correct decisions require leaders willing to lose elections over them.*

Rawls asks what institutions rational people would design. I ask who actually runs them. In November 1979 I pushed the NATO Double-Track decision through a divided SPD and a hostile public. The missiles were right; the polls were wrong. I lost my coalition three years later, partly because of it. No constitutional constraint substitutes for a leader who accepts that duty and popularity are different things. Independent institutions help, but they are staffed by people. The question is whether those people have the character to hold the line.

**Challenge to Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** Does civic virtue form itself, or must someone build it?

## Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Philosopher, Geneva 1750–78

*Short-termism is a symptom of corrupted citizens, not broken institutions.*

Schmidt builds institutions to constrain bad citizens. I ask how the citizens became bad. In Geneva I watched a small republic hold together because its people identified with the common good, not because its magistrates were insulated from them. Representative democracy as it is currently practiced is, as I wrote in 1762, slavery between elections: citizens choose their masters and then go home. The fix is not more insulation from voters. It is forming citizens capable of willing what is genuinely common, not what is merely convenient.

**Challenge to Amartya Sen:** Can public reasoning form itself without civic education first?
---
## Amartya Sen
Economist and philosopher, India/United Kingdom 1970–present

*Democracy fails long-termism when public reasoning fails, not democracy itself.*

Rousseau asks whether citizens can be formed for civic virtue. I ask how. In 1943 Bengal, the colonial administration suppressed information about the famine while people starved. No free press meant no accountability, and no accountability meant no correction. The same logic applies to long-term governance: a democracy without a genuinely free press, without real education in public reasoning, cannot generate the information governments need to act beyond the next cycle. The solution is not to insulate leaders from citizens. It is to make citizens capable of demanding long-term thinking by giving them the tools to see it.

**Challenge to Olof Palme:** Did Sweden's institutions build capable citizens, or just constrain governments?
---
## Olof Palme
Prime Minister, Sweden 1969–76, 1982–86

*Organised civil society makes short-termism structurally costly for any government.*

Sen asks whether Sweden built capable citizens or just constrained governments. Both. The Rehn-Meidner model, which I steered through parliament from 1969, embedded trade unions, employer associations, and cooperative institutions into the policy process itself. When LO sat at the table, wage decisions had a ten-year horizon, not a two-year one. No single government could promise short-term wage inflation without organised labour calling it out. The lesson is not that voters need better reasoning or that leaders need more courage. It is that civil society institutions, when genuinely incorporated into governance, make short-termism expensive before any election arrives.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
The problem is real: electoral cycles systematically discount future costs and benefits. Some institutional buffer between immediate public pressure and long-term decisions is necessary.

**Where it divides:**
Rawls and Schmidt place the fix in institutional design and leadership character respectively, insulating decision-makers from short-term pressure. Rousseau, Sen, and Palme each reject this: Rousseau argues corrupted civic culture is the root cause; Sen argues the fix is expanding public reasoning capacity through press freedom and education; Palme argues organised civil society makes short-termism structurally costly without reducing accountability. Schmidt and Palme also disagree directly: Schmidt trusts individual leaders to absorb punishment for correct decisions; Palme argues no leader should have to stand alone when institutions embed long-horizon actors into the process.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Choose between two concrete designs: independent bodies and constitutional fiscal rules that constrain parliaments, or mandatory incorporation of long-horizon civil society actors, such as unions, future-generations commissioners, and professional associations, into the legislative process before votes are cast.
