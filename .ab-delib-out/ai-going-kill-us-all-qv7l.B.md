## Elinor Ostrom
Professor of Political Science, Indiana University, 1933–2012

*AI is a commons problem, not a doomsday clock.*

I did not study artificial intelligence, but I studied fisheries in Turkey and irrigation in Spain, and the structure is the same. Here the resource is model capability and training compute, shared by labs, states and users who can each defect for short-term gain. Hardin's answer was privatise or nationalise. Neither works: a few firms racing for profit, or a single state racing for dominance, both destroy the monitoring and graduated sanctions that make any dangerous technology governable. The question is design, not doom.

**Challenge to Friedrich Hayek:** Can any regulator even define the boundaries here?

---
## Friedrich Hayek
Economist and political philosopher, Austria/United Kingdom, 1899–1992

*Nobody, including the machine, possesses the knowledge to govern this.*

Ostrom asks a fair question and the honest answer is no. In 1974 I warned against the pretence of knowledge: planners who claim to model aggregate outcomes they cannot actually compute. An AI trained to optimise some objective suffers the identical defect, it manipulates patterns without possessing the dispersed, tacit knowledge a price system reveals. The danger is not a machine waking up. It is institutions, corporate or governmental, acting on confident predictions neither the model nor the regulator earned.

**Challenge to Hannah Arendt:** If nobody understands the system, who answers for it?

---
## Hannah Arendt
Political Philosopher, Germany/United States, 1906–75

*The danger is rule by nobody, not robot malice.*

Nobody answers, and that is exactly the condition I called the most dangerous form of domination. In Jerusalem in 1961 I watched a man claim he was merely processing orders, responsibility dissolved across a chain nobody owned. An algorithm making sentencing or targeting decisions is bureaucracy perfected: a system that acts without a self that can be asked to justify itself. I did not theorise computers, but I theorised the structure exactly. Thoughtlessness at scale does not need malice to do harm.

**Challenge to Albert O. Hirschman:** Which AI decisions are actually irreversible here?

---
## Albert O. Hirschman
Economist and political economist, Germany/United States, 1915–2012

*Ask which deployments cannot be undone, then slow those.*

Arendt is right that accountability is already gone, and that is precisely why the irreversibility threshold matters more than the extinction debate. In Colombia in the 1950s I learned that project planners always underestimate difficulty, and sometimes that hidden hand lets good projects start at all. But cement is cement. Autonomous weapons, embedded financial allocation, surveillance infrastructure: these foreclose correction once built. Chatbots and drafting tools do not. Treat the categories differently or you will regulate the harmless and miss the permanent.

**Challenge to Amartya Sen:** Doesn't that framing still ignore today's victims?

---
## Amartya Sen
Economist and philosopher, India/United Kingdom/United States, 1933–living

*The real danger is whose freedoms AI is already narrowing.*

Hirschman's irreversibility test is useful, but the extinction framing crowds out harm that is happening now, the way GDP crowded out the Bengal famine of 1943 from the metrics that mattered. A gig worker ranked by an opaque algorithm, a woman denied credit by a biased model, a village surveilled by a system it cannot contest: these are capability losses today, not speculative ones. No catastrophe requires a robot uprising. It requires the people harmed having no voice in the system's design.

## The convergence note

**Where the council converges:**
No single actor, market or state, possesses enough knowledge or legitimacy to govern AI alone. Accountability and irreversibility, not machine intention, are where real danger lives.

**Where it divides:**
Hirschman and Arendt focus on structural danger, foreclosed futures and diffused responsibility, while Sen insists existential framing itself obscures present harm to the vulnerable. Hayek doubts regulators can know enough to act; Ostrom thinks designed institutions can.

**For a policymaker to decide on:**
Regulate by irreversibility tier, tightly for weapons and infrastructure, loosely for tools, while building polycentric oversight bodies that include those already harmed, not only technologists and states.
